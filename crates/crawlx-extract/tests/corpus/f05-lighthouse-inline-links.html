The Long Winter of the Skerry Light

For ninety years the keepers of the Skerry Light lived on a rock the size of a football pitch, three miles from the nearest road. The lamp was automated in 1971, and the buildings were left to the gulls. Last autumn a small charity took the lease and began to put the place back together.

Volunteers reached the rock by inflatable boat whenever the swell dropped below a metre, which in the first month was on only four days. They carried slates, lime mortar and a generator up a stair cut into the stone, and slept in the old oil store with a stove that smoked.

The lantern room was the hardest job. Salt had eaten through the brass frames, and two of the glass panes were cracked from top to bottom. A glazier from the mainland spent three weeks replacing them, working only on windless mornings.

By December the kitchen had a roof again. Project leader Dilys Penhallow keeps a list on the wall of everything the team has found beneath the floorboards, including a tin whistle, a ration book and a letter that was never posted.

The charity hopes to open the light to day visitors by next summer, weather permitting. For now it is a work site, and the only audience is the seals that gather on the northern ledge.
